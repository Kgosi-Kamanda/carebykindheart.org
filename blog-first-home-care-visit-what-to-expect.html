What to Expect From Your First Home Care Visit

Stephen Guto, Co-Founder · March 10, 2026 · 5 min read

A lot of the anxiety around starting home care isn't really about the care itself — it's about not knowing what to expect. Who's coming into the house? What will they ask? What if it feels awkward? Here's a plain walkthrough of what actually happens, based on how we run it.

Before the visit

It starts with a phone call, not a form. We ask about the situation in your own words — what's prompting the call, what daily life looks like right now, and what you're hoping changes. There's no script and no pressure to commit to anything. From there, we schedule a free in-home assessment at a time that works for your family.

What happens during the assessment

A care coordinator comes to the home, usually for about 45 minutes to an hour. They'll walk through daily routines — meals, medications, mobility, hygiene, sleep, and anything specific to your situation — and take a look at the physical space for safety considerations. This isn't a test the person has to pass. It's information-gathering, done with the person present and involved wherever possible, not talked over.

By the end, you'll have a written care plan that spells out what kind of support makes sense, how many hours, and what it will cost. You're free to take it and think it over. There's no pressure to sign anything on the spot.

Meeting your caregiver for the first time

We match based on more than availability — skills, personality, and household fit all factor in. Before the first shift, you'll know who's coming and a little about them. The first visit is usually lighter than people expect: getting acquainted, learning the home's routines firsthand, and starting to build the relationship that every good caregiving arrangement depends on.

The first two weeks

Expect some adjustment. Even a caregiver who's a great fit takes a little time to learn the specifics of a household — where things are kept, how someone likes their coffee, what topics spark conversation and which ones don't. Your care coordinator checks in during this period specifically to catch anything that needs adjusting early, rather than waiting for a problem to become obvious.

What if it's not a fit?

Sometimes a caregiver-client match doesn't click, and that's not a failure on anyone's part — it happens. Tell your care coordinator directly. We'd rather hear it early and adjust than have a family quietly tolerate something that isn't working. Consistency matters, but not at the expense of the actual relationship.

"The first visit sets the tone for everything after it. We take that seriously — it's not just a scheduling event, it's the start of trust." — Stephen Guto, Co-Founder
