Disability Support at Home: Building Independence, Not Dependence

Felister Ndugire, Co-Founder · March 28, 2026 · 6 min read

There's a version of "help" that quietly takes over, and a version that builds capability. The difference matters enormously for adults with disabilities, where the goal isn't to be cared for in the way an infant is cared for — it's to have the support that makes independence realistically possible.

Independence isn't the same as doing everything alone

This is worth saying plainly: needing support with some tasks doesn't make someone less independent. Independence is about having control over your own life — your schedule, your choices, your relationships — not about managing every physical task without help. A caregiver who assists with transfers, meal prep, or transportation isn't taking independence away. Done well, they're what makes independence possible in the first place.

What support looks like day to day

For adults with physical, developmental, or cognitive disabilities, in-home support can include:

  • Personal care and mobility assistance, done in a way that respects dignity and preference
  • Help with meal planning and preparation, geared toward building skills where that's a goal, not just completing the task
  • Transportation to work, appointments, or community activities
  • Medication reminders and health routine support
  • Companionship that reduces isolation without becoming the only social connection someone has

The specific mix depends entirely on the individual. That's the point — support built around a diagnosis alone almost always fits poorly. Support built around a person fits.

Matching support to the person, not a diagnosis

Two people with the same diagnosis can need completely different kinds of support. One person's priority might be maintaining their job and needing transportation reliability. Another's might be building daily living skills after a recent injury. Our assessments focus on the person's actual goals and daily reality, not a generic care template stamped with a diagnosis code.

Working alongside other providers

Home care rarely exists in isolation. Many of the adults we support also work with occupational therapists, case managers, or other specialists. We see our role as complementing that team, not operating separately from it — consistent communication with other providers, when families want that, is part of doing this well.

Questions families ask us most

  • "Will the same caregiver come every time?" Yes — we match a primary caregiver based on skills, personality, and schedule, and aim to keep that relationship consistent.
  • "Can support change as needs change?" Yes. A care plan isn't fixed; it's reviewed and adjusted as circumstances shift.
  • "Is this only for older adults?" No — our disability support services are specifically for adults, at any age, with physical, developmental, or cognitive disabilities.
"The best compliment we get isn't 'you took such good care of him.' It's when someone tells us they got their job back, or started seeing friends again, because the support around them finally fit." — Felister Ndugire, Co-Founder
